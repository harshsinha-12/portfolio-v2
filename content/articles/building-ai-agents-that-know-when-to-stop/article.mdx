## Why “stop” matters

LLMs are brilliant at generating the next step. Agents built on them are brilliant at generating *more* next steps. Without a deliberate stopping strategy, they loop, overthink, spend tokens, and gradually lose the reader’s trust.

Great agents are decisive. They gather enough evidence, act, and stop.

<MarginNote>
  Goal: a correct answer, delivered at the right time.
</MarginNote>

## What we mean by stop

Stopping is not a single event; it is a judgement. Continue only while the expected value of another step is greater than its cost in latency, tokens, money, or risk.

<Callout title="Key idea">
  An agent’s job is not to keep working. Its job is to recognize when the requested outcome is complete—or when continuing is unsafe.
</Callout>

```python
def should_stop(state):
    confidence = estimate_confidence(state)
    progress = state.progress_score
    budget_used = state.tokens_used / state.token_budget

    return confidence >= 0.95 or progress >= 0.95 or budget_used >= 0.90
```

## Designing the stop criterion

A useful stop decision combines multiple signals rather than betting everything on one hard-coded rule:

- confidence in the answer
- measurable progress toward the goal
- time, token, and tool-call budgets
- repeated attempts or stable outputs
- an external validator when correctness can be checked

## A practical pattern

Put an evaluator after every meaningful action. The evaluator should describe what changed, score progress, and select one of three outcomes: continue, deliver, or escalate.

    B --> C[Observe result]

    D --> E{Decision}
    E -- Continue --> A
    E -- Deliver --> F[Respond]
    E -- Escalate --> G[Ask for help]`}
